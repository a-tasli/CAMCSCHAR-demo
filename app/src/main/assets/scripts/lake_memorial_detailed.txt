[1] We are now standing by the expansive artificial lake, which represents the breathtaking ecological heart and hydrological center of the Sabancı University campus.
[2] This massive body of water was ingeniously created by repurposing the deepest crater left behind by the former gravel extraction operations that occupied the land before the university was built.
[3] Engineers meticulously leveled the basin, sealed it with a highly durable impermeable clay lining, and layered it with gravel to create a sustainable and robust aquatic environment.
[4] To fill and sustain this incredible lake, a highly advanced, closed-loop hydrological network was implemented across the entire campus infrastructure.
[5] Rainwater is systematically harvested from the sprawling roofs of the academic and residential buildings and channeled directly into the lake via a complex and efficient piping network.
[6] Crucially, the lake also serves as a terminal reservoir for the campus's treated sewage wastewater, ensuring a fully environmentally sustainable recycling process that minimizes waste.
[7] This remarkable system ensures that even during years of severe regional drought, the lake retains sufficient volume to irrigate the entirety of the campus's lush green spaces.
[8] The ecological health of this artificial environment is so robust that natural aquatic wildlife spontaneously developed within it, including fish populations that were brought in naturally by local seagulls.
[9] Surrounding the tranquil waters of the lake is the university's globally renowned Japanese Ornamental Cherry, or Sakura, tree collection, which proudly stands as the largest of its kind in Turkey.
[10] Between 2007 and 2009, following a profound philanthropic agreement with the Japanese conglomerate Komatsu, two thousand five hundred highly delicate Sakura saplings were transported to Turkey.
[11] Through the intense, multi-year cultivation efforts of dedicated Japanese botanists, nearly two thousand trees successfully took root and now grow over four meters in height across the campus.
[12] This remarkable collection features seven genetically pure species, which paint the campus in brilliant, vivid pinks and whites every late March and early April, perfectly mimicking the historical blooming cycles of Kyoto.
[13] Anchoring this incredible arboricultural achievement is the Sakura Stone Monument, an immense three point three ton stone that is estimated to be ten thousand years old.
[14] This perfectly smooth, round monument was extracted from the Gujyo city area in the Gifu region of central Japan, its unique geometry forged entirely by millennia of relentless river currents.
[15] The stone was specifically selected to symbolize endurance and unyielding resilience, standing as a permanent testament to the strong, enduring relationship between the university and its international partners.
[16] The monument bears the Japanese kanji for "Sakura," masterfully engraved based on the exquisite calligraphy of Toshitaka Hagiwara, former Chairman of Komatsu and President of the Japan Flower Association.
[17] Together, the advanced hydrological lake, the blossoming Sakura trees, and the ancient Gujyo monument create a profoundly peaceful and emotionally resonant space, often serving as a picturesque backdrop for students during their memorable graduation ceremonies.
[18] The visionary engineering behind this artificial lake also plays a critical role in the microclimate regulation of the campus, providing a cooling effect during the sweltering summer months.
[19] Visitors can frequently observe a diverse array of avian species that have made this flourishing ecosystem their permanent sanctuary.
[20] The integration of the Sakura trees alongside the lake was not merely an aesthetic choice, but a deliberate cultural bridge fostering international horticultural exchange.
[21] Every spring, the spectacular blooming of the Sakura flowers draws scholars, artists, and nature enthusiasts from across the region to witness this fleeting natural phenomenon.
[22] The Gujyo Sakura Stone Monument, standing resolutely amidst the delicate falling petals, provides a poetic contrast between the permanence of stone and the ephemeral nature of life.
[23] This entire sector of the campus serves as a testament to the belief that human engineering, when executed with profound respect for nature, can heal and elevate the environment.
